2. Civil Administration

Part of the role of the DOJ is to facilitate the day to day administration requests for the city, these include - Name Changes, Marriage Registration, Divorce, Annulments, Will Management, Medical Power of Attorneys, Expungement, Pardons and Business Partnerships.

A. Name Change Requests

  • The Petitioner files for a name change
  • Any judge or court clerk may process the name change request.
  • Judge or clerk confirms that there are no active BOLOs/Arrest Warrants for the person–if there are, the change is denied Judge or clerk confirms that there are no violent felonies in the last 14 days - if there are, the request must be passed to a Judge only.
  • The judge will use their best judgment to ensure that the petitioner is not trying to get a name change for purposes of fraud or any other potentially illegal reason
  • If joining a family, they must have permission to join the family
  • If Denied, advise the petitioner why they were denied and let them know they can petition the courts again for the same matter in one week's time.
  • If Accepted, the judge will implement the name change.

B. Marriage Registration

Marriage registration is important in the event that one partners passes away without a will - the registered partner will be entitled to 100% of the estates properties, vehicles and Banking

  • A marriage can be registered between any two persons
  • The state does not recognise polygamy in a formal setting, a partner may have one registered partnership and as many unregistered as they wish. It is noted that the unregistered have no claim on an estate in the event of a death.
  • The Petitioner files for marriage
  • A judge or court clerk requests confirmation from the co-petitioner and the officiant that this is consensual and this marriage did happen.
  • A judge or clerk of the court will then must determine that
  • The marriage was consensual
  • The ceremony did occur (any person can be an officiant - no formal registration is needed)
  • Neither the petitioner nor the co-petitioner is already in a Registered Marriage
  • If a hearing is required to determine this, a $500 administration fee will be invoiced to the Judiciary funds
  • Any name change is reasonable and relevant to the marriage - a separate request is not necessary.
  • If Denied, the petitioner may correct any errors in procedure and apply again immediately.
  • If Accepted, the judge or clerk of the court must mark the paperwork indicating it was accepted. Update the Marriage Register and issue the Certificate of Marriage, then if a name change has been approved, a judge will implement the name change.

C. Divorce

A divorce is only required in the matter of Registered Marriages and requires a single party to register the desire to dissolve the marriage

  • One of the parties on the Marriage Registration files for the dissolution.
  • The dissolution must include details on the reasoning and any request for allocation of assets.
  • The Judge needs to confirm if any pre-nuptial agreements are in place that may override any requests on the dissolution
  • If division of assets is required, a hearing will be held to determine the requested allocation, if this is not possible due to abandonment then the asset split will be awarded
  • Any name change is reasonable and relevant to the divorce and not too long (25 characters maximum)
  • If Denied, the petitioner may correct any errors in paperwork and attempt again in one week.
  • If Accepted, the judge or clerk of the court must mark the paperwork indicating it was accepted, update the Marriage and Divorce registry, and issue the certificate of dissolution. If a name change has been approved a judge will implement the name change. If assets need to be reallocated this will be handled by an SCJ

D. Annulment

An annulment (unlike a divorce) is where a Marriage Registration is canceled and is as if it has never taken place. This is only completed on the grounds of Fraud of one of the parties

  • One of the parties on the Marriage Registration files for the annulment
  • The annulment must include details on the reasoning for the request.
  • A hearing will need to be heard with the petitioner to determine if an annulment is valid - or if a divorce is suitable
  • There are no divisions of assets with an annulment - regardless of any prenups in place. Both parties leave the marriage in the same state they entered into
  • Both Parties would revert back to any prior names.
  • If Denied, the petitioner may correct any errors in paperwork and attempt again in one week.
  • If Accepted, the judge or clerk of the court must mark the paperwork indicating it was accepted, update the Marriage and Divorce registry, and issue the certificate of dissolution. If a name change has been approved a judge will implement the name change.

E. Will Management

A will is a living document, once created and accepted by the court it can be updated at any time by the requester - however must be completed formally with either a Court Clerk or a Judge,

  • The Petitioner completes the Will Application and submits it to the Court
  • A judge or court clerk reviews the request and ensures that it has not been made under duress
  • If a hearing is required to determine this, an administration fee will be invoiced to the Judiciary funds.
  • The document is validated to ensure that any items being bequeathed are within the city limits (no sharing between characters on one account)
  • If Denied, the petitioner may correct any errors in paperwork and attempt again in one week.
  • If Accepted, the judge or clerk of the court must mark the paperwork indicating it was accepted, and update the Will register.

F. Medical Power of Attorney

A medical power of attorney is a signed document giving one or two agents (persons) the ability to make decisions in regards to your medical care in the event that you are not able to.

  • The Petitioner completes the Medical Power of Attorney and submits it to the Court
  • A judge or court clerk reviews the request and ensures that it has not been made under duress, that all agents understand the significance of what they have agreed to do.
  • If a hearing is required to determine this, a $500 administration fee will be invoiced to the Judiciary funds.
  • If Denied, the petitioner may correct any errors in paperwork and attempt again in one week.
  • If Accepted, the judge or clerk of the court must mark the paperwork indicating it was accepted and update the petitioner's MDT profile to show it is in place. A copy of the document must also be provided to the SAHA command for the person's medical records.

G. Expungements

An Expungement is the process of sealing one or more crimes on an individual’s criminal record that can be

granted on a case by case basis by a Judge to individuals that have proven a pattern of reform. Capital crimes

can not be expunged from a record and requires a request for Pardon.

How long do I have to wait for Expungement?

  • Although there is no definitive amount of time to wait, enough time should have passed for the Judge to determine a pattern of reform.
  • Any request submitted less than 14 days of the last misdemeanor or non-violent felony criminal conviction will be denied.
  • Any request submitted less than 30 days of the last violent felony criminal conviction will be denied.

Do I need a lawyer?

  • You do not technically need a lawyer to assist with this, however, if you are unsure on the process or on what to write as your statement it may be beneficial to consult with one.

What does the process look like?

  • After applying for expungement, a member of the judiciary will be assigned to handle the expungement. The Judge will look at the provided material and determine if there has been enough evidence of reform to proceed and if a hearing is needed to be scheduled, or if it can be completed “offline”

What will happen if I need a hearing?

  • The hearing will be a place where the individual requesting expungement will testify and answer questions asked by the Judge. There will also be time allotted for anyone to testify in support or in opposition to the expungement.

What will happen if I don't need a hearing?

  • The Judge may contact you to ask questions, or they may simply process on the Docket - this usually happens in the case of very old requests where the pattern of reform is clear and the crimes are non violent in nature.
  • Any Charges that your expungement relates to will be removed from your record and a cost will be issued (anywhere from $0 to $250,000 dependent on the number and severity of charges)

What happens if the Expungement is denied?

  • You may refile in two weeks if you have new information to present.

What kind of evidence should I provide?

  • You will be required to complete a personal statement when making the submission, stating why you wish to change and what you have done to demonstrate a change in behavior
  • All Expungements are done on a case by case basis, but recommendations from prominent citizens such as Police Officers, Doctors, Government Officials, and Business Owners as well as evidence of having taken part in Community Service will help prove a pattern of reform.

How long will an expungement take?

  • The Expungement process will follow the typical appeals process except it will be presided over by a single judge as opposed to a panel.
  • If the paperwork is completed offline - this is usually completed in 7-14 days dependent on Judicial workload
  • If a hearing is needed and more evidence required - it can take up to 30 days

What happens if it is approved?

  • Your charges will be removed from your record and the date and approving Judge will be added to your records
  • A fee between $0 to $250,000 will be charged, this is based on the number and complexity of charges and will be added to any state debt
  • If you have requested your weapons license to be reinstated - this will be reactivated and you will need to see a Law Enforcement Officer to gain your license back.

H. Pardons

A Pardon is reserved for those who have been found guilty of capital crimes, it is not an easy process and will

take time to complete.

How long do I have to wait to request a pardon?

  • A minimum of 6 months from the time of your arrest

Do I need a lawyer?

  • Yes, a lawyer will be required to help you navigate the extensive application

What does the process look like?

  • After applying for a pardon, a Justice will review the application and you will be called for a hearing. This hearing will ask you about every aspect of the documentation prepared - you will need to have character witness as well as employee references to be considered.
  • The Justice will then take some time to complete background checks and deliberate if a pardon is suitable

What happens if the Pardon is denied?

  • You can reapply after 1 month, new evidence would be required to support your new application

What happens if it is approved?

  • Your charges will be removed from your record and the date and approving Judge will be added to your records
  • A fee between $100,000 to $500,000 will be charged, this is based on the number and complexity of charges and will be added to any state debt
  • If you have requested your weapons license to be reinstated - this will be reactivated and you will need to see a Law Enforcement Officer to gain your license back

I. Business Partnerships

A Business Partnership is a legally binding agreement that states two persons have 50% equal rights and responsibilities in relation to any business.

  • The Owner files the registration with the Court
  • A clerk or Judge reviews the business register to ensure that there are no existing partnerships registered (if there are - a dissolution must be filed)
  • If Denied, the petitioner may correct any errors in paperwork and attempt again in one week.

If Accepted, the judge or clerk of the court must mark the paperwork indicating it was accepted, update the Business register and issue the certificate of registration. The owner is responsible for ensuring that the BBB receives a copy of the certificate.

3. Criminal Administration - Court Theory

A. Reasonable Suspicion vs Probable Cause and False Arrest

In San Andreas, we use both Reasonable Suspicion and Probable Cause as conditions for questioning and detainment in regards to criminal actions. So, what's the difference?

There’s a simple way to determine whether evidence is cause for probable cause or reasonable suspicion: the standard for probable cause is that any reasonable person might suspect criminal activity, while reasonable suspicion exists if any reasonable Officer might suspect it.

In its most basic form, Reasonable suspicion is the suspicion of criminal activity, and probable cause is the proof of criminal activity.

Reasonable Suspicion

Reasonable suspicion is a legal standard used in criminal law that refers to a belief or suspicion by law enforcement that a person may be involved in criminal activity based on specific and articulable facts and circumstances. It is a lower threshold than probable cause but higher than a mere hunch or speculation.

In essence, reasonable suspicion allows law enforcement officers to briefly detain an individual for investigative purposes if they have a reasonable belief, supported by objective facts, that the person is engaged in criminal activity, is about to engage in criminal activity, or is otherwise connected to a crime.

Factors that may contribute to reasonable suspicion include observed behavior, information from witnesses or informants, the location and time of day, the individual's known criminal history, and other relevant factors. However, it's important to note that reasonable suspicion does not require the same level of certainty as probable cause and is based on an officer's reasonable interpretation of the circumstances.

Ultimately, reasonable suspicion serves as a legal basis for law enforcement to initiate a brief investigatory stop or detention, such as a traffic stop or a Terry stop (stop and frisk), to further investigate the situation and determine whether there is probable cause to make an arrest or conduct a more thorough search.

Probable Cause

Probable cause is a legal standard used in criminal law to determine whether law enforcement officers have sufficient reason to believe *that a crime has been, is being, or is about to be committed*, thereby justifying certain actions such as making an arrest or conducting a search. It represents a higher level of certainty than reasonable suspicion but does not require absolute certainty.

In essence, probable cause exists when there are specific facts and circumstances that would lead a reasonable person to believe that a crime has occurred or that evidence of a crime may be found in a particular place. These facts and circumstances must be more than mere suspicion or speculation but do not need to reach the level of certainty required for a conviction.

Probable cause can be established through various means, including witness statements, physical evidence, law enforcement observations, expert opinions, or information obtained through informants. It serves as a crucial safeguard against unreasonable searches and seizures under the Fourth Amendment of the United States Constitution and similar legal principles in other jurisdictions.

Once probable cause has been established, law enforcement officers may take certain actions, such as obtaining a warrant to search a premises or arrest a suspect. However, the determination of probable cause is ultimately a legal question that may be subject to review by a court.

Examples of Reasonable Suspicion vs Probable Cause:

Scenario 1 - House Party: A group of young adults is attending a party at a friend's house. The music is loud, and there's a lot of laughter coming from inside. Some neighbors hear the noise and suspect that underage drinking might be happening at the party. They call the police to report their suspicions.

  • Reasonable Suspicion:

The police arrive at the scene based on the neighbor's report. They don't have enough evidence to arrest anyone yet, but they have reasonable suspicion that underage drinking might be occurring inside the house. The noise, the number of people coming in and out, and the fact that it's a gathering of young adults all contribute to this suspicion.

  • Probable Cause:

As the police approach the house, they smell alcohol coming from the open door. This provides them with probable cause to enter the premises and investigate further. The smell of alcohol, combined with the earlier suspicion reported by the neighbors, gives the police officers reasonable grounds to believe that a crime is being committed – in this case, underage drinking.

Once inside, the officers observe several teenagers holding cups that appear to contain alcoholic beverages. This further confirms their suspicions and provides additional probable cause to take action, such as issuing citations or making arrests.

Scenario 2 - Potential Drug Sales:

A group of young adults is hanging out in a park known for recreational activities. A couple of individuals have been observed by park-goers to be engaging in suspicious behavior, such as exchanging small packages and frequently looking around nervously.

  • Reasonable Suspicion:

A concerned citizen notices this behavior and reports it to the police, expressing their reasonable suspicion that drug sales might be taking place in the park. The police officers arrive at the park to investigate based on this report. The suspicious behavior observed by the citizen, such as the exchange of small packages and nervous demeanor, contributes to the officers' reasonable suspicion that illegal activity might be occurring.

  • Probable Cause:

As the police officers observe the individuals in question, they notice further signs that support the suspicion of drug sales. For example, they see one individual handing cash to another in exchange for a small package. This exchange, along with the previous reports and observations, provides the officers with probable cause to detain the individuals and conduct a search.

During the search, the officers find a significant quantity of illegal drugs packaged for sale on one of the individuals. This discovery confirms their suspicions and provides clear probable cause for making arrests and pursuing charges related to drug trafficking.

Scenario 3 - Traffic Stop

Two police officers are patrolling a highway known for its high rate of speeding and reckless driving. They notice a car weaving in and out of lanes at a high speed, and it appears to be exceeding the speed limit by a significant margin.

  • Reasonable Suspicion:

The police officers observe the erratic driving behavior of the car, which includes frequent lane changes without signaling and speeding well above the posted limit. Based on these observations, they have reasonable suspicion to believe that the driver may be impaired, distracted, or driving recklessly.

  • Probable Cause:

The officers decide to initiate a traffic stop on the vehicle. As they pull the car over, they notice additional signs that support their suspicion. For example, they detect the smell of alcohol coming from the driver's breath as they approach the vehicle. Additionally, they observe the driver's eyes appearing bloodshot and glassy.

These additional observations, combined with the initial erratic driving behavior, provide the officers with probable cause to further investigate the possibility of impairment. They may conduct field sobriety tests or request a breathalyzer test to determine if the driver is under the influence of alcohol or drugs.

If the tests confirm impairment, the officers would then have probable cause to make an arrest for driving under the influence (DUI) or related offenses.

Why is Probable cause not enough for a court conviction?

Probable cause is not enough for a criminal conviction because it represents a lower threshold of certainty compared to what is required to *prove guilt beyond a reasonable doubt* in a criminal trial.

In a criminal trial, the *prosecution must prove the defendant's guilt beyond a reasonable doubt*, which is the highest standard of proof in the legal system. This means that there must be *no reasonable doubt* in the minds of the judiciary that the defendant committed the crime with which they are charged.

Probable cause is, however, a standard used in pre-trial proceedings, such as obtaining search warrants or making arrests. It is based on a reasonable belief that a crime has been, is being, or is about to be committed, supported by specific facts and circumstances. Probable cause does not require the same level of certainty as proof beyond a reasonable doubt.

Because probable cause is a lower standard, it does not provide sufficient evidence to support a criminal conviction. While it may justify certain actions by law enforcement, such as making an arrest or conducting a search, it does not establish guilt to the degree required for a conviction in a court of law. Instead, probable cause is just the initial step in the legal process, and the prosecution must present additional evidence and meet the higher burden of proof at trial to secure a conviction.

False Arrest

False arrest refers to the unlawful detention or confinement of an individual by law enforcement or another authority without proper legal justification or probable cause. It occurs when someone is wrongly deprived of their freedom by being detained, arrested, or held against their will without valid legal grounds.

Examples of False Arrest:

Mistaken Identity:

A person is arrested and detained by police due to mistaken identity. For example, they may share a name or physical resemblance with a suspect wanted for a crime, but they are not actually involved in any wrongdoing.

Unlawful Search and Seizure:

Law enforcement officers conduct a search without a warrant or probable cause and then arrest someone based on evidence obtained illegally. For instance, an officer enters a person's home without permission or proper justification, finds drugs during the search, and arrests the homeowner without valid legal grounds.

Excessive Use of Force:

In some cases, individuals may be falsely arrested due to the excessive use of force by law enforcement officers. For example, if an officer uses excessive force during an arrest that is not warranted by the circumstances or the individual's actions, resulting in an unlawful arrest.

Retaliation or Discrimination:

False arrest can occur as a result of retaliation or discrimination by law enforcement or other authorities. For instance, an individual may be arrested as a form of retaliation for exercising their legal rights, such as freedom of speech or assembly, or based on their race, ethnicity, or other protected characteristics.

Improper Legal Procedures:

An individual may be falsely arrested if law enforcement fails to follow proper legal procedures, such as failing to inform them of their rights (Miranda rights), or if there is insufficient evidence to support the arrest.

B. The Warrants Process

A warrant is needed when law enforcement officials require authorization from a member of the Judiciary to conduct certain actions that would otherwise violate an individual's constitutional rights. The issuance of a warrant serves as a safeguard against unreasonable searches and seizures, as guaranteed by the Fourth Amendment of the United States Constitution.

Here are some common situations when a warrant is typically required in our city:

Search Warrants:

A search warrant is needed when law enforcement officials intend to conduct a search of a person's property, such as a home, vehicle, or place of business, in order to seize evidence related to a crime. The warrant must specify the place to be searched and the items to be seized, and it must be based on probable cause.

Arrest Warrants:

An arrest warrant is required when law enforcement officials intend to arrest a specific individual for a suspected crime. The warrant must be based on probable cause and must identify the individual to be arrested and the offense for which they are being arrested.

Wiretap Warrants:

In cases involving electronic surveillance law enforcement officials need a warrant issued by a judge or magistrate. This warrant must demonstrate probable cause and specify the scope and duration of the surveillance.

Warrants for Blood Draws:

In cases where law enforcement officials need to obtain a blood sample from an individual, a warrant will be required to perform the blood draw if the individual does not consent to it voluntarily.

Capias Warrants:

A capias warrant is issued when someone needs to be detained to ensure that they make a scheduled court appearance. If a defendant has been released on bail, the defendant is expected to show up for their scheduled court date. If the defendant does not show up, then the bond is revoked and the courts will issue a capias warrant for the defendant’s arrest

Summons:

A summons must be in the same form as a warrant except that it must require the defendant/witness to appear before a judge at a stated time and place.

It's important to note that there are some exceptions to the warrant requirement, such as exigent circumstances (emergency situations where obtaining a warrant would be impractical - typically used for Medical warrants when a defendant has been admitted to hospital or for Jailbreaks - in these cases a warrants is still requested, however it can be acted upon before a Judge has authorized it), searches incident to a lawful arrest, consent searches, and certain administrative searches. However, in most cases involving searches and seizures, a warrant is required to ensure that law enforcement actions comply with constitutional protections against unreasonable government intrusion

4. Criminal Administration - Before Court

A. Pleading Options

In criminal cases, defendants have several plea options available to them, each carrying different legal consequences and implications for the outcome of the case. These plea options serve as the defendant's formal response to the criminal charges they face.

Guilty Plea:

A guilty plea is an admission by the defendant that they committed the crime(s) charged. By entering a guilty plea, the defendant accepts responsibility for the offenses and waives their right to a trial. They will be fined for the crimes and potentially sent to prison (based on the charges applied)

In some cases, a guilty plea is accompanied by an agreement with the prosecution, known as a plea bargain or plea agreement. This agreement typically involves the defendant agreeing to plead guilty to certain charges in exchange for concessions from the prosecution, such as reduced charges, a lesser sentence, or dismissal of other charges.

When a person enters a Guilty plea and is sent to prison, that is the equivalent of a criminal case being concluded - as such, the charges against the defendant can not be changed in relation to that crime unless new evidence has come to light - this is the concept of Double Jeopardy.

Double jeopardy is a legal principle that protects individuals from being tried or punished twice for the same offense. The concept is derived from the Fifth Amendment of the United States Constitution, which states: "nor shall any person be subject for the same offense to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb." Double jeopardy prohibits the government from prosecuting an individual more than once for the same offense. Once a person has been acquitted or convicted of a particular crime, they cannot be tried again for that same offense, regardless of the outcome of the first trial.

While double jeopardy generally prevents retrial for the same offense, there are exceptions to this principle. For example:

  • If there is a mistrial declared due to a procedural error, the prosecution may be allowed to retry the case
  • If the defendant successfully appeals their conviction and it is overturned, they may be retried for the same offense.

Double jeopardy protections apply only to the specific offense or offenses for which a person has been convicted. They do not prevent separate prosecutions for different offenses arising from the same conduct i.e. in the same incident such as a Bank Robbery they could be charged with both Robbery and Assault or Evading.

Not Guilty Plea:

A not guilty plea is a denial by the defendant that they committed the crime(s) charged. By entering a not guilty plea, the defendant asserts their innocence and requires the prosecution to prove the charges beyond a reasonable doubt at trial.

A not guilty plea preserves the defendant's right to a trial. It allows the defendant to challenge the prosecution's evidence and present their own defense. They will be sent to prison for the time of the charges - but they will NOT be fined. A defendant may change their plea at any time in the court process up to the point of confirmation of the plea in Court.

Nolo Contendere (No Contest) Plea:

A nolo contendere plea, commonly known as a no contest plea, is neither an admission of guilt nor a denial of the charges. Instead, the defendant does not contest or dispute the charges.

Unlike a guilty plea, a no contest plea does not admit fault or liability, but it has the same legal effect as a guilty plea for sentencing purposes. The defendant accepts the consequences of the plea without admitting guilt.

A no contest plea is often used in situations where the defendant faces potential civil liability for the same conduct, as it may limit the use of the plea as evidence in subsequent civil proceedings.

For example, if a victim sues the defendant for pain & suffering or medical bills, they don't want a guilty plea to make them liable for damages. They will NOT have the opportunity to contest the charges in a criminal court trial. They will be fined and sent to prison to serve their sentence.

B. Cell Representation

When a criminal is in police custody, they have the right to legal representation as long as someone is available. Here's what typically happens:

Notification of Rights: Upon arrest, individuals are informed of their rights, which include the right to remain silent and the right to legal representation. This notification is often referred to as the Miranda rights.

Request for Legal Representation: If a person is arrested and placed in police cells, they have the right to request legal representation. They may do so by informing the police officers or investigators of their desire to speak with a lawyer.

Contacting an Attorney: The defendant in police custody may have the opportunity to contact an attorney of their choice. They may do so using a phone provided by the police or by requesting that the police contact an attorney on their behalf.

Duty Counsel: In some cases, individuals may be provided with legal representation through a duty counsel . These services offer free legal assistance to individuals.

Consultation with Attorney: Once legal representation is arranged, the attorney may visit the individual in police custody to provide legal advice and assistance. The attorney will review the circumstances of the arrest, advise the individual on their rights, and discuss potential legal strategies.

Representation during Questioning: If the police wish to question the individual while they are in custody, their attorney may be present during the interrogation to ensure that their rights are protected and to advise them on how to respond to questioning.

Negotiating a Plea: Defense lawyers or Public Defenders are able to negotiate times up to 50% when asked to attend the cells for a representation. It is noted that Law Enforcement Officers do not need to agree to this, however if this is a recurring pattern examples must be given to the Judiciary for escalation

C. Bail

The purpose of bail in the legal system is to allow individuals accused of a crime to secure their temporary release from custody pending trial, while ensuring their appearance in court and the safety of the community. Bail serves several important functions within our criminal justice system:

Presumption of Innocence:

Bail upholds the principle of "innocent until proven guilty" by allowing individuals accused of crimes to remain free while awaiting trial. It prevents pretrial detention from punishing individuals before they have been convicted of a crime.

Ensuring Appearance in Court:

Bail provides an incentive for defendants to appear in court for their scheduled hearings and trial dates. By requiring individuals to post bail as a condition of release, the court aims to mitigate the risk of defendants fleeing or failing to appear in court, thus ensuring the integrity of the judicial process.

Protecting Public Safety:

Bail conditions may include requirements such as electronic monitoring, supervision by Law Enforcement Officers, or restrictions on contact with alleged victims or witnesses. These conditions help to mitigate potential risks posed by the defendant to public safety or the community.

Usage of Bail:

Typically Bail is only granted for those on long sentences, such as a Hold Until Trial or multiple counts of Murder - it can be granted for smaller periods of time, however the practicalities and arrangements limit it to typically sentences of 1 year +

Defining & Processing Bail:

In our state, Bail can only be granted by a judge or a member of the District Attorney’s Office. Bail is not an automatic right - if a member of the Judiciary deems that Bail is to be refused it can not be contested.

If Bail is to be considered, a bail hearing must be convened, where the Judiciary/DAO will decide based on a number of factors:

  • Flight risk - is the person likely to try and run and avoid the court process and any consequences
  • The type of crime - the nature of the crime and circumstances around it
  • Danger to the public - do they pose a continued threat to the public at large

The Judge or DAO may stipulate certain bail conditions if granted - for example, that they refrain from entering a certain area, participating on certain activities such as drugs or alcohol, wear an ankle monitor, or other modifiers which restrain the liberties of a defendant while released

A. Court Process: The Purpose of Criminal Court

The purpose of the criminal justice system is multifaceted, encompassing various goals and objectives aimed at maintaining social order, ensuring public safety, upholding the rule of law, and promoting justice and fairness. Key areas that are considered are:

Public Safety and Crime Prevention:

One of the primary purposes of the criminal justice system is to protect the public from criminal activity and maintain community safety. Through law enforcement, prosecution, and punishment of offenders, the system aims to deter criminal behavior and prevent future crimes.

Upholding the Rule of Law:

The criminal justice system serves to enforce and uphold the rule of law by holding individuals accountable for violating legal norms and societal standards. It ensures that laws are applied fairly and consistently to all members of society, regardless of their background or status.

Punishment and Rehabilitation:

The criminal justice system seeks to impose appropriate sanctions, such as fines, probation, incarceration, or rehabilitation programs, on individuals who have been found guilty of committing crimes. These sanctions serve various purposes, including punishment, deterrence, rehabilitation, and societal reintegration.

Fairness and Due Process:

Central to the criminal justice system is the principle of due process, which guarantees defendants' rights to a fair and impartial trial, legal representation, and protection against arbitrary or unjust treatment. The system strives to ensure that defendants are treated fairly and afforded their constitutional rights throughout the legal process.

Victim Support and Redress:

The criminal justice system aims to provide support and assistance to crime victims by offering services such as counseling, compensation, and legal advocacy. It seeks to address the needs of victims, protect their rights, and facilitate their participation in the legal proceedings.

Restorative Justice and Community Healing:

In addition to traditional punitive measures, the criminal justice system increasingly emphasizes restorative justice approaches that focus on repairing harm, promoting accountability, and fostering healing for all parties involved, including victims, offenders, and communities affected by crime.

Accountability and Rehabilitation:

While holding offenders accountable for their actions, the criminal justice system also recognizes the importance of rehabilitation and reintegration into society. It provides opportunities for offenders to address underlying issues, such as substance abuse, mental health disorders, or socioeconomic challenges, and to develop the skills and support networks needed to lead law-abiding lives.

In Summary, the purpose of the criminal justice system is to maintain public order, protect individual rights, promote fairness and equity, and facilitate the resolution of criminal cases in a manner that serves the interests of justice and the well-being of society as a whole.

Court Structure:

The courts of San Andreas are broken into two separate courts with an upper court of appeals. The two main courts are the Civil Courts and the Criminal Courts. Both of these courts deal with different types of legal dispute but the overall process is the same. The District Court is staffed by Judges, these will hear any type of criminal case with the exception of Capital Cases. B. Court Process: Pre-Filings

In criminal court proceedings, a "pre-filing" refers to the period before formal charges are brought against a defendant. During this phase, law enforcement agencies and the district attorney's office may conduct preliminary investigations, gather evidence, and assess the circumstances of a case to determine whether criminal charges should be filed.

Investigation:

Law enforcement agencies and prosecutors use the pre-filing stage to investigate allegations of criminal activity thoroughly. This may involve collecting witness statements, examining physical evidence, conducting forensic analysis, and gathering other relevant information to determine whether there is sufficient evidence to support criminal charges.

Assessment of Evidence:

During the pre-filing stage, prosecutors evaluate the evidence gathered during the investigation to assess its strength and reliability. They consider factors such as the credibility of witnesses, the admissibility of evidence, and the likelihood of securing a conviction at trial.

Legal Analysis:

Prosecutors analyze the applicable laws and statutes to determine whether the evidence supports the elements of a crime and whether there is a legal basis for filing criminal charges against the defendant. They consider factors such as the severity of the alleged offense, the potential impact on victims, and any mitigating or aggravating circumstances.

Decision-Making:

Based on the results of the investigation and legal analysis, prosecutors make informed decisions about whether to proceed with filing criminal charges against the defendant. This decision involves weighing the strength of the evidence, the interests of justice, and the likelihood of obtaining a successful outcome in court.

Negotiation and Resolution:

In some cases, prosecutors may engage in pre-filing discussions with defense attorneys to explore the possibility of resolving the case through plea negotiations. These discussions may occur before formal charges are filed to expedite the resolution of the case and minimize the burden on the criminal justice system.

Timings

A pre-filing must be submitted by the arresting officer (or a designated LEO) to the DAO within 72 hours of the arrest of the defendant. If there is no submission within 72 hours, the case will be dismissed with prejudice. The DAO has 7 days to complete the assessment as detailed above, if the deadline passes (and no request for an extension has been made to the judiciary in advance), the case will be dismissed with prejudice

Next Steps If the DAO is satisfied that the case holds merit, the pre-filing will be filed with the Court as a Docket and the case will move forward. If the opposite is true, the DAO will dismiss all charges, the defendant's criminal record for this will be cleared and any monies paid returned. It is good practise that the DAO informs the arresting officer of the reason for the decision not to continue.

Acceptance by the Court:

The case is reviewed by a member of the Judiciary and is assigned - at this stage, the Judiciary does NOT make any assessment as to the validity of the charges and the evidence. All cases are accepted into the court process.

F. Court Process: Pre Trial Proceedings - Discovery

After the acceptance, the case enters the pretrial phase, during which various pretrial proceedings may occur. The largest of these is “Discovery”

Discovery in a criminal case refers to the process by which the prosecution and defense exchange information and evidence relevant to the case. The purpose of discovery is to ensure that both parties have access to all available evidence, allowing for a fair and transparent trial.

Initial Disclosure:

Both the prosecution and defense are required to provide an initial disclosure of certain basic information without a formal request. This may include the names and contact information of witnesses, a summary of witness statements, and any evidence or documents intended to be introduced at trial.

Formal Requests:

In addition to the initial disclosure, either party may request specific information, documents, or evidence from the other party through formal discovery requests. These requests may take various forms, such as interrogatories (written questions), requests for production of documents or tangible evidence, and requests for admission of facts.

Compliance and Response:

The party receiving a discovery request is generally required to respond within a specified timeframe, either by providing the requested information, objecting to the request, or asserting any applicable privileges or protections (e.g., attorney-client privilege). Subpoenas issued by a Judge have the full effect and weight of Law.

Failure to answer a Subpoena may result in Contempt of Court and Obstruction of Justice. Government Officials and Law Enforcement are not excluded.

Witness Statements and Reports:

Discovery often includes witness statements, police reports, investigative notes, and other documentation related to the case. Both the prosecution and defense may request copies of these materials to review and prepare their respective cases, these should be given freely

Evidence Sharing:

Both parties are required to disclose and share physical evidence, such as weapons, DNA samples, forensic reports, photographs, and any other items relevant to the case. This allows each side to inspect and analyze the evidence and potentially challenge its admissibility at trial.

Depositions:

A deposition is a legal proceeding conducted outside of court in which witnesses or parties to a lawsuit provide sworn testimony, usually in the form of answers to questions posed by attorneys. The testimony is given under oath and is recorded. They are usually used when a party may be unable to attend court at the given time - or in the case of Confidential Informants if the identity is at risk of being revealed.. Both sides and the judge must be present for the deposition, and any questions are subject to objection if appropriate. A Defendant in a Criminal Trial can not be forced to testify in any form against themselves and therefore immune to Interrogatory and Deposition requests.

Expert Witnesses:

If either party intends to call expert witnesses to testify at trial, they must disclose the identity of these witnesses, along with their qualifications and opinions. The opposing party may then have the opportunity to depose or cross-examine these witnesses and review any reports or findings prepared by them.

Court Supervision:

All requests in relation to discovery must be made on the court docket and will be supervised by the assigned judge, they will resolve any disputes or objections raised by the parties regarding the scope or adequacy of the discovery responses.

G. Court Process: Pre Trial Proceedings - Evidence

Evidence in criminal court refers to information, materials, or testimony presented to the court by the prosecution or defense to support their respective arguments regarding the guilt or innocence of the defendant. This evidence may take various forms, including physical objects, documents, witness testimony, expert opinions, and digital or forensic evidence. The purpose of presenting evidence is to establish facts relevant to the case and to persuade the judge or jury of the defendant's culpability or lack thereof. The rules governing the admissibility and presentation of evidence in criminal court are designed to ensure fairness, reliability, and the pursuit of truth in the administration of justice.

There are four types of evidence allowed in San Andreas that can be proven or disproven at trial.

Real Evidence:

Real evidence, often called physical evidence, consists of material items involved in a case, objects and things the judge can physically hold and inspect. Examples of real evidence include fingerprints, blood samples, a knife, a gun, and other physical objects. Real evidence is usually admitted because it tends to prove or disprove an issue of fact in a trial. Real evidence is usually involved in an event central to the case, such as a murder weapon, clothing of a victim, narcotics or fingerprints. In order to be used at trial, real evidence must be relevant, material, and authentic. The process whereby a lawyer establishes these basic prerequisites is called laying a foundation, accomplished by calling witnesses who establish the item's chain of custody.

Demonstrative Evidence:

Demonstrative evidence, usually charts and diagrams, demonstrate or illustrate the testimony of a witness. It's admissible when it fairly and accurately reflects the witness's testimony and is more probative than prejudicial. Maps, diagrams of a crime scene, charts and graphs that illustrate physical or financial injury to a plaintiff are examples of demonstrative evidence. Witnesses create and use demonstrative evidence at trial, and opposing counsel may use the same evidence to prove contrary positions.

Testimonial Evidence:

When a person gets up on the stand at trial and relates something that they saw or heard, that is testimonial evidence. It is simply a witness giving testimony under oath about the facts of the case.

Documentary Evidence:

The production of documents at trial is documentary evidence which is presented to prove or disprove certain allegations at trial. These documents can be from a vast number of sources from diaries, letters, contracts, newspapers, and any other type of document that you can think of. There are restrictions and qualifications for using documents at trial as there is a need to make sure they are authentic and trustworthy.

Restrictions and Qualifications of Evidence:

To ensure the authenticity and trustworthiness of documents used as evidence at trial, certain restrictions and qualifications must be met. These measures help safeguard the integrity of the judicial process and ensure that only reliable evidence is presented to the court

  • Authentication: Documents must be properly authenticated to establish their authenticity and reliability. This often involves presenting evidence or testimony to demonstrate that the document is what it purports to be and was created or maintained in the ordinary course of business. There may be requirements for authenticating documents, such as certification, notarization, or testimony from witnesses familiar with the document's creation or contents.
  • Chain of Custody: For physical documents or tangible evidence, the chain of custody must be established to trace the handling and storage of the evidence from the time it was obtained until its presentation in court. This helps ensure that the document has not been tampered with or altered.
  • Foundation: Attorneys must lay a foundation for the admission of documents into evidence by establishing their relevance and reliability. This may involve questioning witnesses or presenting other evidence to support the authenticity and trustworthiness of the documents.
  • Hearsay Rule: The hearsay rule prohibits the admission of out-of-court statements offered for the truth of the matter asserted, unless an exception applies. Documents containing hearsay statements may be excluded from evidence unless they fall within a recognized exception to the hearsay rule.
  • Best Evidence Rule: The best evidence rule requires that the original document or a reliable duplicate be presented as evidence when the content of the document is at issue. Copies or secondary evidence may be admitted if the original is unavailable or if certain exceptions apply.
  • Expert Testimony: In some cases, expert testimony may be required to interpret or explain the contents of complex documents, such as financial records, scientific reports, or forensic evidence. Expert witnesses can help establish the reliability and significance of the documents.
  • Authentication Procedures: Courts may have specific procedures or requirements for authenticating documents, such as certification, notarization, or testimony from witnesses familiar with the document's creation or contents.

Probative vs Prejudicial:

"More probative than prejudicial" is a legal standard used to assess the admissibility of evidence in a criminal case. It means that the probative value of the evidence, or its tendency to prove a fact that is relevant to the case, outweighs any potential prejudice it may cause to the opposing party. In other words, the evidence must be more helpful in determining the truth of the matter at issue than harmful to the fairness of the proceedings.

  • Probative Value: Probative value refers to the degree to which evidence tends to prove or disprove a fact that is relevant to the case. Evidence with high probative value is directly relevant to the issues in dispute and helps the Judge to understand what happened and make an informed decision.
  • Prejudicial Effect: Prejudicial effect refers to the potential harm or unfairness that the evidence may cause to the opposing party. This could include inflaming the emotions of those present, unfairly portraying the defendant in a negative light, or leading to a decision based on improper considerations rather than the merits of the case.

When determining whether evidence is more probative than prejudicial, the court weighs the relevance and importance of the evidence against its potential to undermine the fairness of the trial. If the probative value of the evidence substantially outweighs any prejudicial effect, the evidence may be admitted. However, if the prejudicial effect of the evidence outweighs its probative value, the court may exclude it.

This standard reflects the balancing act that courts must perform to ensure that trials are fair and that the truth-seeking process is not compromised by the introduction of evidence that is unfairly prejudicial or misleading. It aims to promote the search for truth while protecting the rights of the accused and ensuring the integrity of the legal process

H. Court Process: Pre Trial Proceedings - Motions

In criminal court proceedings, motions in pre-trial hearings are formal requests made by either the prosecution or the defense to the court regarding various legal issues or procedural matters that arise before the trial begins. These motions are filed in writing and presented to the judge on the docket, where the court will consider the arguments made by both parties before making a ruling.

Motion to Suppress Evidence:

This motion requests the court to exclude certain evidence from being admitted at trial. Grounds for suppression may include violations of the defendant's constitutional rights, such as illegal searches and seizures, Miranda violations, or coerced confessions.

Motion to Dismiss Charges:

This motion asks the court to dismiss all or some of the charges against the defendant. Grounds for dismissal may include insufficient evidence, prosecutorial misconduct, violations of statutory or constitutional rights, or other legal defects in the charging documents.

Motion for Discovery:

This motion requests the prosecution to provide the defense with copies of all evidence and information relevant to the case. Discovery may include police reports, witness statements, laboratory reports, physical evidence, and any other materials that may be used by the prosecution at trial.

Motion for Continuance:

This motion asks the court to postpone or reschedule a scheduled hearing or trial date. Grounds for a continuance may include the need for additional time to prepare the defense, the unavailability of key witnesses, or other unforeseen circumstances that would make it impractical or unfair to proceed with the trial as scheduled.

Motion to Sever:

In cases involving multiple defendants or multiple charges, this motion requests the court to separate or sever the trials to prevent prejudice or confusion. Grounds for severance may include conflicts of interest between co-defendants, evidence that is admissible against one defendant but not another, or other factors that would make a joint trial unfair.

Motion for Judgement: The State may motion for a Default Judgment (automatic guilty verdict) when the defendant has emigrated from the state - or a Summary Judgment (the case is judged based on the evidence submitted) when the defendant or legal representation has made no contact with the docket by the end of Discovery.

I. Court Process: Pre Trial Proceedings - Franks Hearing

A Franks hearing in a criminal case is a proceeding held to determine the validity of a search warrant when the defendant alleges that the warrant was obtained based on false or misleading information provided by law enforcement officers. The name "Franks hearing" originates from the U.S. Supreme Court case Franks v. Delaware (1978), which established the legal standard for challenging the veracity of information contained in a search warrant affidavit.

The following is how we utilize a Franks hearing:

Basis for Challenging the Warrant: In order to initiate a Franks hearing, the defendant must present specific allegations and evidence demonstrating that the search warrant affidavit contained false statements or omissions made knowingly and intentionally or with reckless disregard for the truth.

Request for a Hearing: The defendant's attorney files a motion with the court requesting a Franks hearing to challenge the validity of the search warrant. The motion must include a sworn affidavit setting forth the alleged false statements or omissions and the reasons why they are believed to be inaccurate or misleading.

Presentation of Evidence: At the Franks hearing, the defense presents evidence, such as witness testimony, documents, or other materials, to support the claim that the search warrant affidavit contained false information. The prosecution may also present evidence in response.

Burden of Proof: The defendant bears the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence that the false statements or omissions in the search warrant affidavit were made knowingly and intentionally or with reckless disregard for the truth. This is a relatively high standard of proof.

Legal Standard: The court applies the legal standard established in Franks v. Delaware, which requires the defendant to show that, if the false statements or omissions were removed from the affidavit, the remaining information would not establish probable cause to issue the search warrant.

Decision by the Court: Based on the evidence presented at the Franks hearing, the court determines whether the defendant has met the burden of proof required to suppress the evidence obtained as a result of the search warrant. If the court finds that the search warrant was invalid due to false statements or omissions, it may suppress the evidence obtained through the search.

Open and closed court sessions refer to whether the public is allowed to attend and observe the proceedings of all trials, pre- and post-trial evidentiary proceedings, arraignments, bond hearings, plea proceedings, sentencing proceedings, or portions thereof):

Open Court Sessions:

In open court sessions, members of the public, including the media, are generally allowed to attend and observe the proceedings. This openness promotes transparency in the judicial process, fosters public trust in the legal system, and ensures accountability of judges and attorneys. It helps to uphold the principles of justice, fairness, and the rule of law by allowing the public to witness legal proceedings firsthand.

However, certain exceptions may apply, such as cases involving sensitive information, safety concerns, or national security concerns, where the court may restrict public access.

Closed Court Sessions:

In closed court sessions, access to the proceedings is restricted to only those directly involved in the case, such as the parties, attorneys, witnesses, and court personnel. Closed sessions may be ordered by the judge for various reasons, including protecting sensitive information, preserving the privacy of individuals, or ensuring the safety of witnesses or jurors. While closed sessions limit public access, they are sometimes necessary to protect the integrity of the legal process, maintain confidentiality, and safeguard the rights of the parties involved.

Rationale:

The State should take a position on any motion to close a judicial proceeding, and should ordinarily oppose closure; it should move for or consent to closed proceedings only when closure is plainly essential to the interests of justice. The following must be considered when assessing the decision to close a case;

  • Closure is clearly likely to prevent the harm sought to be avoided;
  • The degree of closure is minimized to the greatest extent possible;
  • The public is given adequate notice of the proposed closure; and, in addition, the motion for closure is made on the record, except where the disclosure of the details of the motion papers would clearly defeat the reason for closure;
  • Transcripts of the closed proceedings will be unsealed as soon as the interests requiring closure no longer obtain; and
  • Failure to close the proceedings will produce;
  • A substantial likelihood of denial of the right of any person to a fair trial; or
  • A substantial likelihood of imminent danger to the safety of parties, witnesses, or other persons; or
  • A substantial likelihood that ongoing investigations will be seriously jeopardized.
  • The Judiciary will have the final say on if a case will be closed to the public and are responsible for posting publicly that court is closed

Q. Court Process: The Trial - Opening Statements

An opening statement in a criminal court is an oral presentation made by attorneys representing the prosecution and defense at the beginning of a trial. The prosecution will always go first, followed by the Defense. It serves as an introduction to the case and provides an overview of each side's theory of the case, the evidence they expect to present, and the arguments they intend to make to the judge or jury. There is no requirement that you must have an opening statement, however it is recommended as it is used to set the scene for what is to come.

The different stages of an opening statement in a criminal court:

Introduction: The attorney delivering the opening statement introduces themselves, their client (if applicable), and their role in the case. They may also briefly outline the charges against the defendant.

Overview of the Case: The attorney provides a general overview of the case, including a summary of the events leading up to the alleged crime, the relevant background information, and any key facts or circumstances that will be important for understanding the case.

Theory of the Case: The attorney outlines their theory of the case, explaining how they believe the evidence will show that the defendant is guilty or innocent. They may highlight the main points they intend to prove or refute during the trial.

Description of Evidence: The attorney previews the evidence they plan to present during the trial, including witness testimony, physical evidence, documents, and other exhibits. They may discuss how each piece of evidence supports their argument and reinforces their theory of the case.

Persuasive Argument: The attorney may use persuasive language and storytelling techniques to engage the judge and convey the importance of their client's position. They may emphasize key themes, credibility of witnesses, or inconsistencies in the opposing party's case.

Cautionary Note: Attorneys often remind the judge that the opening statement is not evidence and should not be considered as such. They may also caution against making premature judgments or forming opinions before hearing all the evidence presented during the trial.

Conclusion: The attorney concludes their opening statement by summarizing the key points they have made and reiterating their client's position. They may also express confidence in their case and the evidence they plan to present.

Overall, the opening statement in a criminal court sets the stage for the trial by providing a roadmap of each side's case and outlining the issues that will be explored during the proceedings. It is an opportunity for attorneys to make a favorable impression on the judge and to lay the groundwork for presenting their evidence and arguments during the trial.

R. Court Process: The Trial - Being a Witness

Being a good witness in court is essential for ensuring that testimony is clear, credible, and effectively communicates the facts relevant to the case. Areas to think about are:

Tell the Truth: This is the most fundamental aspect of being a witness. Always tell the truth, even if it may be uncomfortable or inconvenient. Lying under oath can have serious legal consequences.

Listen Carefully to the Questions: Pay close attention to the questions asked by the attorneys and the judge. Make sure you understand the question before answering, and if you're unsure, don't hesitate to ask for clarification.

Speak Clearly and Confidently: When giving your testimony, speak clearly and confidently. Project your voice so that everyone in the courtroom can hear you, and avoid mumbling or speaking too quickly.

Stay Calm and Composed: Courtrooms can be intimidating environments, but it's important to remain calm and composed while giving your testimony. Take deep breaths if you feel nervous, and try to maintain a steady demeanor.

Be Respectful: Show respect to the judge, attorneys, and other courtroom personnel at all times. Address the judge as "Your Honor" and wait for permission before speaking. Avoid interrupting others while they're speaking.

Answer Only What's Asked: Stick to answering the questions asked of you, and avoid volunteering additional information unless specifically requested to do so. Keep your responses concise and to the point.

Be Honest About What You Don't Know: If you don't remember certain details or if you're unsure about something, it's okay to say so. Don't try to guess or speculate, as this can undermine your credibility as a witness. Lying under oath or affirmation is a crime in the state of San Andreas, the penalties apply to knowingly providing false or inaccurate information whilst on the stand as a witness or on a sworn affidavit (if you are unable to attend court).

Be Respectful of the Court's Rules and Procedures: Familiarize yourself with the court's rules and procedures before your testimony. Follow any instructions given to you by the judge or attorneys, and comply with any courtroom etiquette requirements.

Dress Appropriately: Dress in clean, professional attire for your court appearance. Your appearance can contribute to the impression you make as a witness.

Stay Focused: Keep your focus on the case at hand and avoid distractions in the courtroom. Maintain eye contact with the person asking you questions and try to block out any external distractions.

S. Court Process: The Trial - Hostile Witness

A hostile witness in court is a witness who exhibits a hostile or uncooperative attitude towards one of the parties or their attorney during their testimony. Hostility can manifest in various ways, including being argumentative, evasive, unresponsive, or openly antagonistic towards the attorney questioning them. Hostile witnesses may intentionally withhold information, provide contradictory statements, or attempt to undermine the case of the party calling them.

When dealing with a hostile witness, the attorney questioning them has several options:

Clarification: The attorney can attempt to clarify the question or rephrase it in a way that encourages a more cooperative response from the witness.

Confrontation: The attorney may choose to confront the witness about their behavior and remind them of their obligation to provide truthful and relevant testimony.

Impeachment: If the witness contradicts their earlier statements or provides inconsistent testimony, the attorney can impeach the witness by pointing out these inconsistencies to the judge.

Leading Questions: attorneys are allowed to ask leading questions to a hostile witness. Leading questions are those that suggest a particular answer, and they can help control the direction of the testimony and elicit the desired information.

Refresh Memory: If the witness claims not to remember certain details, the attorney may attempt to refresh their memory by showing them documents, photographs, or other evidence related to the case.

Motion to Declare Hostile: In extreme cases, the attorney may request that the judge declare the witness as hostile. This designation allows the attorney to ask leading questions and may provide other advantages in confronting the uncooperative witness.

Overall, the treatment of a hostile witness depends on the circumstances of the case. Attorneys must balance the need to challenge uncooperative testimony with the duty to maintain professionalism and adhere to ethical standards during the proceedings.

T. Court Process: The Trial - Evidence submission and Witness testimony

After opening statements in a criminal court, the trial proceeds with the presentation of evidence by both the prosecution and the defense. Here's what typically happens after opening statements:

Prosecution's Case-in-Chief: The prosecution presents its case-in-chief by calling witnesses and introducing evidence to support the charges against the defendant. Witnesses may include eyewitnesses to the alleged crime, law enforcement officers involved in the investigation, experts, and other individuals with relevant knowledge or information. Evidence presented by the prosecution may include physical evidence, such as weapons or forensic samples, documentary evidence, and any other exhibits relevant to the case.

It is key to note that the burden of proof is on the prosecution to prove their case. On criminal matters to secure a conviction, there must be beyond a reasonable doubt that the accused did the crimes to be found guilty of the charges. Beyond a reasonable doubt is the highest burden of proof in our legal system.

Examination and Cross-Examination: After each witness testifies, the attorneys have the opportunity to question the witness through direct examination (for the party who called the witness) and cross-examination (for the opposing party). Direct examination allows the attorney to elicit testimony from the witness that supports their case, while cross-examination provides an opportunity to challenge the witness's credibility, reliability, or the accuracy of their testimony. This is limited to questioning the witness about the answers given under Direct Examination by the Prosecution. If some important information is revealed during Cross Examination, the Court may provide the Defense some leeway to explore so long as it is relative to the charges against the Defendant, the truthfulness of the witness, or the discovery of previously unknown facts, evidence, or material witnesses.

Neither side shall interrupt the other’s questioning of a witness unless they immediately verbalize their objection to the Court based upon a rule of evidence or procedure being heard only to the extent of the grounds for the objection. The Court will rule on any objections. Once the ruling is made, there is no further argument on the points of law contained in the Objection. Late objections may be denied (see next section for more details).

Once the direct examination and the cross examination of a witness has been completed, the prosecution will have the opportunity to provide immediate rebuttal to the cross examination.

Once the Prosecution has presented all of the witnesses and evidence in the matter, The Prosecution will rest its case

Presentation of Defense: After the prosecution rests its case, the defense has the opportunity to present its case by calling witnesses and introducing evidence to refute the charges and/or raise affirmative defenses.

The defense may present witnesses who provide alibis, challenge the prosecution's evidence, or offer alternative explanations for the events in question. Like the prosecution, the defense also conducts direct examination of its witnesses and may face cross-examination by the prosecution.

Once the direct examination and the cross examination of a witness has been completed, the defense will have the opportunity to provide immediate rebuttal to the cross examination.

Once the Defense has presented all of the witnesses and evidence in the matter, The Defense will rest its case

U. Court Process: The Trial - Objections

Procedural law is established to protect the rights of the individuals in a court case against badgering, slander, inflammatory statements or allegations, and irrelevant / immaterial. How the system works is much like a competitive sports match. During a direct examination, both parties may present an Objection to a certain line of questions that they feel are either irrelevant, slanderous, leading, badgering, or misleading to the evidence presented. If the objecting party can give grounds to their objection (briefly mind you), the judge will either respond with a sustained or overruled.

If an objection is made, questioning and any witness response MUST STOP until a ruling is made on the objection. Parties must briefly explain why or why not an objection should be sustained, however, if they begin arguing then a ruling should be made immediately to keep the trial progressing. When in doubt, you can just have the party rephrase a question.

Objections are great tools for both prosecution and defense in controlling a direct or cross-examination of a witness, and a Judge should be very familiar with them all in order to Sustain (approve) or Overrule (reject) an objection.

Objections fall into two categories - those from Questions and those from Answers.

Question - Leading: this is when an attorney asks a question that suggests the answer or prompts the witness to agree with a particular statement. Leading questions are generally prohibited during direct examination but are permitted during cross-examination. Examples are:

  • Isn't it true that you saw the defendant at the scene of the crime
  • You were with the victim on the night of the incident, weren't you?
  • You agree that the defendant was acting in self-defense, correct?
  • The defendant never attempted to flee from the police, did he?
  • You were not able to clearly see the suspect's face, were you?

In each of these examples, the attorney is asking a question that suggests a specific answer or prompts the witness to agree with a particular statement rather than allowing the witness to provide their own testimony freely. This type of questioning can potentially influence or bias the witness's response and is therefore objected to as leading.

Question - Speculation: this occurs when one party objects to testimony or argument that is based on conjecture, guesswork, or speculation rather than facts or evidence presented in the case. Examples are:

  • Witness Testimony - "Based on the defendant's previous behavior, I believe he must have committed the crime."
  • Interrogation of Witness - "Do you think the defendant could have committed the crime, even though there is no direct evidence linking him to the scene?
  • Expert Testimony - "In my professional opinion, the defendant is likely innocent because I don't believe he had a motive to commit the crime."
  • Expert Testimony - "Although there is no physical evidence tying the defendant to the crime, it's possible he could have committed it."
  • Hearsay Statement - "I heard from a friend that the defendant confessed to committing the crime."
  • Characterization of Evidence - "Based on the defendant's behavior during the interrogation, it's clear he was trying to cover up his involvement in the crime."

In each of these examples, the objection is raised to prevent the introduction or consideration of speculative statements, opinions, or questions that lack a factual basis or are not grounded in the evidence presented during the trial. This helps maintain the integrity of the trial process and ensures that decisions are based on reliable evidence and legal principles rather than speculation or conjecture.

Question - Argumentative:. An argumentative objection occurs when an attorney objects to a statement made by the opposing counsel on the basis that it is argumentative rather than a proper form of questioning or argumentation. Examples are:

  • The attorney is making statements that are intended to provoke an argument or engage in debate with the witness rather than eliciting relevant testimony through proper questioning.
  • The attorney's question contains assumptions or assertions that have not been established by the evidence presented in the case, and it is presented in a confrontational or argumentative manner.

The examples illustrate how attorneys may object to argumentative statements or questioning during a criminal trial to ensure that the proceedings remain focused on relevant evidence and legal arguments, and to prevent unnecessary confrontation or confusion.

Question - Calls for conclusion: In a criminal case, a "calls for conclusion" objection occurs when an attorney makes a statement during trial that draws a conclusion or expresses an opinion about the guilt or innocence of the defendant without presenting evidence to support that conclusion. Examples include:

  • Witness Testimony - "Mr. Smith, based on your observations at the scene of the crime, would you say it's fair to conclude that the defendant is the one who committed the burglary?"
  • Witness Testimony - Ms. Johnson, based on what you saw that night, would you say it's fair to conclude that the defendant was the one who started the altercation?"
  • Witness Testimony - Officer Jones, would you agree that there's no real evidence tying my client to the crime scene?
  • Witness Testimony - "Mr. Brown, in your opinion, is it possible that someone else committed the crime besides my client”

In each of these examples, the objection is raised because the attorney is asking the witness to draw a conclusion or express an opinion about the defendant's guilt or innocence without presenting evidence to support that conclusion. This type of questioning can be misleading to the court and is considered improper because it bypasses the requirement to establish facts through evidence-based testimony.

Question - Compound question: occurs when an attorney asks a witness a question that contains multiple distinct inquiries or calls for multiple answers within a single question. Compound questions are generally objectionable because they can confuse the witness, lead to unclear or inconsistent responses, and make it difficult for the court to discern which part of the question the witness is responding to. Examples can include:

  • Witness testimony - “Did you see the defendant running away from the scene of the crime and did you hear any sounds of a struggle while you were on the phone with the police?"
  • Witness testimony - "Were you at the bar with the defendant on the night of the incident and did you notice anything unusual about his behavior that evening?”
  • Witness Testimony - "Did you see the defendant enter the building and did you notice if he was carrying anything suspicious when he went inside?"
  • Witness Testimony - "Did you witness the defendant driving the car and did you notice if the vehicle was speeding when it passed by?"

In each of these examples, the compound questioning objection highlights the need for the attorney to ask separate, clear, and focused questions to elicit specific information from the witness. This helps ensure that the witness's responses are accurate, relevant, and comprehensible to the court.

Question - Assumption: this occurs when an attorney asks a question that assumes facts not yet established by the evidence or makes unwarranted assumptions about the witness's knowledge or testimony. These objections are made to prevent misleading or speculative questioning and to ensure that the evidence presented is based on facts supported by the record. Common examples include:

  • Witness testimony - "Since you were at the scene of the crime, you must have seen the defendant commit the robbery, isn't that correct?"
  • Witness testimony - You were present when the drugs were found in the defendant's possession, so you must have known about his involvement in drug trafficking, correct?"
  • Witness testimony - "Given your experience as a police officer, you must agree that the defendant's behavior was consistent with someone attempting to flee the scene of a crime, right?"
  • Witness testimony - "Since you were in the vicinity of the crime scene, you must have seen the defendant's accomplice, correct?"
  • Witness testimony - “"Given your relationship with the victim, you must have known about the defendant's plans to harm them, correct?"”

The assumption objection serves to highlight the need for questions to be based on facts established by the evidence and to prevent speculative or misleading questioning. This helps ensure that the testimony presented is accurate, reliable, and supported by the record.

Question -Hearsay: this is when an attorney attempts to introduce an out-of-court statement made by someone other than the witness testifying, to prove the truth of the matter asserted in that statement. Hearsay is generally considered inadmissible because it lacks the opportunity for cross-examination and may be unreliable. For example:

  • “I would like to present a recording of a conversation between the defendant and a third party, where the defendant admits to the crime”.
  • "The witness heard someone yell 'I did it\!' while walking past the crime scene."
  • "The witness received a text message from a friend saying they saw the defendant with the stolen property."
  • "I would like to introduce a letter written by the defendant's accomplice, in which they implicate the defendant in the crime."

The hearsay objection serves to challenge the admissibility of evidence that relies on out-of-court statements made by individuals who are not present to testify and be subject to cross-examination. Hearsay objections help ensure the reliability and fairness of the evidence presented in a criminal case.

Question -Inflammatory: This is when an attorney attempts to introduce evidence, questioning, or arguments that are likely to arouse strong emotions or prejudices in the judge or other participants in the trial. These objections are made to prevent the presentation of evidence that may unfairly prejudice the case or sway the decision-makers based on emotion rather than the merits of the case. Examples may include:

  • "Your Honor, I want to introduce graphic photographs of the crime scene depicting the victim's injuries, to shock you and elicit sympathy for the prosecution's case."
  • "The defendant has a history of violent behavior and was previously convicted of a similar crime, indicating a pattern of criminal conduct."
  • "The defendant is a dangerous individual who poses a threat to society and must be punished to the fullest extent of the law."
  • "The defendant's actions were callous and depraved, showing a complete disregard for human life."
  • "The defendant's ethnicity and cultural background are irrelevant to this case, but they have a history of criminal behavior typical of their community."

The inflammatory objection serves to challenge the admissibility of evidence, statements, or arguments that are likely to inflame emotions, prejudice the case, or unfairly influence the judge. These objections help ensure that the trial remains fair and impartial and that the outcome is based on the merits of the case rather than emotional manipulation.

Question -Misleading:a misleading objection occurs when an attorney attempts to introduce evidence, questioning, or arguments that are likely to mislead the judge or other participants in the trial about the true nature of the case or the evidence presented. These objections are made to prevent the presentation of misleading information that may unfairly influence the outcome of the trial. Examples include:

  • “I want to introduce a witness who will testify that they saw the defendant at the scene of the crime, but they may not be able to recall specific details due to the trauma of the incident."
  • "The defendant's prior misdemeanor conviction for theft is evidence of their propensity to commit the current offense."
  • “The prosecution failed to introduce any evidence proving the defendant's innocence, so the judge should find the defendant guilty."
  • "The defendant's refusal to testify at trial is evidence of their guilt and consciousness of guilt."

In each of the above examples, the misleading objection serves to challenge the accuracy or fairness of evidence, arguments, or statements presented during the trial. These objections help ensure that the trial remains fair and impartial and that the outcome is based on the merits of the case rather than misleading information.

Question -Incompetent: Comes about when an attorney objects to statements that are being made by a witness because the believe the witness is not qualified to answer the specific question - simple examples are:

  • "Can you explain whether the defendant's actions constitute a violation of the law?" (If a non legal expert witness)
  • "Can you provide an opinion on the cause of death based on your observations?" (if a non medical expert witness)

Question -Irrelevant: Objections for irrelevance in a criminal case are made when questions or evidence presented are not connected to the issues being tried or are not pertinent to the matter. For example:

  • "Did the defendant attend a protest three years ago?"
  • "I want to introduce the defendant's financial records from ten years ago."
  • "Do you know if the defendant has any siblings?"
  • "Did the victim have any pets?"

In each of the examples, the objections are raised because the questions or evidence presented do not contribute to proving or disproving the elements of the alleged crime or any relevant defenses. Typically a Judge may allow some leeway in these questions to see if the attorney has a point that they are trying to get to. Ensuring relevance helps maintain the focus of the trial on the pertinent issues and prevents the introduction of distracting or extraneous information.

Question -Asked and Answered: This is when in a criminal case an objection is made when the same question has already been asked and sufficiently answered during the proceedings. Occasionally an attorney may “harp on” at the point by using this tactic, a judge will usually order them to move along. Simple examples can include:

  • Attorney "Did you witness the altercation between the victim and the defendant?" Witness: "Yes, I did." Later in the same examination: Attorney: "Can you confirm that you saw the altercation between the victim and the defendant?"
  • Attorney: "Were you present at the scene when the crime occurred?" Witness: "Yes, I was there." Later in the same examination: Attorney: "Can you testify to your presence at the scene when the crime occurred?"
  • Attorney: "Did you see the defendant leave the building shortly after the incident?" Witness: "Yes, I observed the defendant leaving the building." Later in the same examination: Attorney: "Can you describe what you saw when the defendant exited the building after the incident?"

In each of these examples, the objection of "asked and answered" is raised to prevent redundant questioning and to maintain the efficiency of the trial proceedings. Once a question has been asked and adequately answered, repeating it serves little purpose other than to waste time.

And finally….

Question -Badgering: Go on, try it… see what happens

Answers - Narrative: This is when a witness provides lengthy or unnecessary explanations beyond the scope of the question asked, potentially introducing irrelevant or prejudicial information. For example:

  • Attorney: "Did you witness the altercation between the victim and the defendant?" Witness: "Yes, I saw the altercation. It was a sunny day, and I was walking down the street when I noticed a crowd gathered near the corner. As I got closer, I saw the victim and the defendant arguing loudly. They were both shouting and shoving each other..."
  • Attorney: "Can you describe what you observed at the scene of the incident?" Witness: "Well, it was a cold morning, and I remember I had just finished my breakfast when I heard a commotion outside. I went to the window and saw..."
  • Attorney: "What did the defendant say to you when they were arrested?" Witness: "The defendant started by telling me about their difficult childhood, how they struggled to make ends meet, and how they were innocent of the charges. They went on to explain their personal problems and family issues..."

In each of these examples, the objection of "narrative" is raised to prevent witnesses from providing lengthy, extraneous, or irrelevant information that may confuse the judge or distract from the relevant issues in the case. It helps to keep the testimony focused and within the bounds of what is necessary to answer the question asked. It is noted that in the city, this is usually not sustained as for the sake of telling a story in an RP situation the benefit of the doubt may be given.

Answers - Non Responsive: Quite simply, this is when the Witness has failed to respond or has responded in a manner that is not relevant to the question being asked. The Judge will then direct the witness to answer the question. Random examples could include:

  • Attorney: “What day of the week did this happen on” Witness “They were wearing an orange shirt”
  • Attorney: “Can you explain what happened” Witness “remains silent..”

The judge will ensure direction is given and is one of only two times when they will intervene with a witness.

V. Court Process: The Trial - Closing Statements

After both Prosecution and Defense have rested, the Trial enters Final Arguments/Closing Statements. It is common at this point for the Judge to ask if the State and Defense are ready to move forward or if they require time to amend their closing statements. Whilst the State and Defense will have spent a great deal of time in advance preparing their case, the closing statement will most likely need to be altered based on what has or has not been said during proceedings. The Judge will allow a short recess for both sides to prepare, during this period, the judge will remove themselves from the courtroom and people are free to talk etc.

When both sides are ready to continue, the prosecution will start and will conduct a Final argument summing up the evidence and the testimony of the witness with the aim of convincing the Court that the evidence is sufficient to find the Defendant guilty of the charge(s). The Defense will then conduct a Final argument with the aim of convincing the Court that the evidence is insufficient to support a finding the Defendant guilty of the charge(s).

The Closing statement is the last narrative that the Judge will hear before leaving the courtroom entering into deliberations, as such, areas to consider are:

Summarize the Evidence: Provide a concise overview of the evidence presented during the trial, highlighting key testimony, exhibits, and facts that support your position. Remind the judge or jury of the most compelling evidence that supports your case.

Highlight Themes and Storytelling: Craft a compelling narrative that weaves together the evidence into a coherent and persuasive story. Emphasize overarching themes that support your theory of the case and resonate with the judge or jury on an emotional level.

Address Weaknesses in the Opposing Case: Acknowledge any weaknesses or inconsistencies in the opposing party's case, but also explain why they do not undermine the overall strength of your argument. Anticipate counterarguments and preemptively refute them.

Rebut Arguments Made by the Opposition: Respond to any arguments made by the opposing party during their closing argument. Clearly explain why their interpretation of the evidence is flawed or why their legal theories are unpersuasive.

Appeal to Reason and Emotion: Appeal to both the logical reasoning and the emotions of the judge. Present logical arguments supported by evidence, but also appeal to their sense of justice, fairness, and empathy.

Focus on the Legal Standards: Remind the judge of the legal standards that apply to the case and explain how the evidence presented meets those standards. Clearly articulate the legal elements that must be proven for a verdict in your favor.

Be Credible and Trustworthy: Maintain your credibility and trustworthiness throughout your final argument. Avoid exaggeration or hyperbole, and present your case in a sincere and authentic manner.

Provide a Clear Call to Action: Clearly articulate the verdict that you are seeking and explain why it is justified based on the evidence and the law. Encourage the judge to render a verdict in your favor by providing a compelling rationale for doing so.

Use Visual Aids Effectively: Consider using visual aids such as charts, diagrams, or photographs to illustrate key points and enhance the clarity of your argument. Visual aids can help reinforce your message and make complex information more accessible.

Leave a Lasting Impression: End your final argument with a memorable closing statement that leaves a lasting impression on the judge. Summarize your key points, reiterate your request for a favorable verdict, and thank the judge for their attention and consideration.

When both sides have completed the deliberations, the Judge will place the court into recess and enter deliberations.

W. Court Process: The Trial - Deliberations

The deliberation process is when the Judge leaves the courtroom and will retire to chambers to consider everything that has been heard before. It involves careful consideration of the evidence presented during the trial, application of the relevant legal principles, and ultimately reaching a decision on the guilt or innocence of the defendant. Before a case comes before the Court a judge will read very little of the information presented and will rely solely on the information provided in the session itself. The judge does not have the luxury of being able to revisit the conversations and as such must keep detailed notes on what they have heard and key points of interest.

It is not unusual for a secondary judge to be present in deliberations, this can assist the primary on reminder of any key points that were made and is often used for training purposes.

Deliberation can take anything from 10 minutes to 30 minutes - based on the case itself. The steps a judge will go through are:

Reviewing the Evidence: The judge begins by reviewing all the evidence presented by both the prosecution and the defense during the trial. This includes witness testimony, physical evidence, exhibits, and any other relevant materials.

Applying the Law: The judge then applies the applicable legal standards and principles to the facts of the case. This involves analyzing the relevant statutes and case law to determine the elements of the crime charged and the burden of proof required for conviction.

Assessing Credibility: The judge evaluates the credibility and reliability of the witnesses who testified during the trial. This includes considering factors such as consistency of testimony, demeanor on the witness stand, and any potential biases or motives for dishonesty.

Weighing the Evidence: The judge weighs the strength and significance of the evidence presented by both sides. This involves determining which evidence is most credible, persuasive, and relevant to the issues in the case.

Reaching a Decision: Based on their review of the evidence and application of the law, the judge reaches a decision on the guilt or innocence of the defendant. If the defendant is found guilty, the judge will also determine the appropriate sentence based on sentencing guidelines and mitigating or aggravating factors. The penal code of San Andreas is a guideline that is used for sentencing and is usually followed by a Judge. A justice however has the right to step outside these guidelines based on the information provided - however, this can not be excessive as it would be in breach of the defendants 8th amendment right.

Drafting the Verdict: Once the decision is reached, the judge drafts a written verdict outlining the findings of fact and conclusions of law.

Throughout the deliberation process, the judge must remain impartial and objective, basing their decision solely on the evidence presented and the applicable law. They must also ensure that the defendant's constitutional rights are protected and that the trial is conducted fairly and in accordance with legal procedures. Often an outcome of a case can swing based on a single sentence uttered or a comment on a piece of evidence. The role of the Judge is to present the outcome and they do not side with the State or Defense.

X. Court Process: The Trial - The Ruling

When the Judge has come to their verdict, they will return to the courtroom and declare that court is now back in session. They will confirm that the State, Defense and Defendant are all present and ready to hear the verdict.

The judge's ruling is based on a careful consideration of the evidence presented during the trial, the applicable law, and legal precedent. The judge applies legal principles to the facts of the case to determine whether the prosecution has proven guilt beyond a reasonable doubt. In most cases, the judge will provide written or oral reasons for their ruling. This helps to explain the legal basis for the decision and ensures transparency in the judicial process.

If the defendant is found NOT GUILTY, the charges will be removed from the defendants record, any fine returned and they “may” be eligible for restitution based on time served in prison.

If the defendant is found GUILTY, the judge imposes an appropriate sentence based on sentencing guidelines, statutory requirements, and mitigating or aggravating factors. The judge may also consider input from the prosecution, defense, and any victims or witnesses.

The Court has considerable leeway when it comes to the penalties that can be given. The following list gives a basic idea of what a person could expect.

Parole - Usually up to 14 days, but no maximum time is set in law. This results in a reduction of freedoms for individuals including random searches. Breaches of parole can result in harsher sentences as well as being ordered to complete the rest of your sentence if released early.

Fines - These are the financial penalties issued to people who are convicted of a crime and are to be paid to the state. The fine will be automatically charged to the person's state debt.

Jail Time and Prison - This is state-sanctioned deprivation of liberty in order to make individuals pay for their crimes whilst also removing them from society in part to protect further victims of their crime. Sentences of jail/prison time are given by the Judge and particular conditions may be set (such as solitary etc). Awarding of time served will be at the discretion of the Court based upon the demeanor of the Defendant since the time of their arrest.

Capital Punishment - Capital Punishment is a sentence of death for an individual. These sentences are never carried out lightly and are only issued to the worst offenders in which there is no hope for rehabilitation, or their continued life poses a greater risk to the people around them.

The judge's ruling, along with any accompanying orders, becomes part of the official court record. It is documented in the court's records and may be subject to review by higher courts if appealed. Once a decision is issued, the case is over and there is to be no further discussion with the Court.

All of the decisions made by the courts are final and can only be altered or overturned on the back of an appeal. Appeals are not just a method for overturning verdicts you do not like, but there must be some evidence of procedural failures or that the trial was conducted in an unfair or prejudiced manner or later being able to prove some illegality in the ruling or the case itself.

Y. Court Process: Bench Trial

A Bench Trial follows the exact same process as a regular court case -just completed very very quickly - a bench trial should start within 30 minutes of one being requested. Any defendant entering a plea of Not Guilty may request a bench trial ( and they must give permission for it to be held)and the Judiciary will attempt to accommodate one. There are a few factors that must be taken into consideration when requesting:

  • A Bench Trial can not be used for Hold Until Trial cases. The seriousness of these crimes require time for both the State and the Defense to prepare arguments, gather evidence etc, it is not deemed feasible for this to take place in an accelerated fashion.
  • A bar certified member of the DAO must be available to represent the state
  • Security must be available to protect the court, if it is not possible the case could be heard in a closed session and the previous mention of public notification is waived.
  • The pre filing must be made from the arrest report and converted to a court docket to allow the defendant access to the evidence
  • It is recommended that the defendant has legal representation, however they may choose to self represent.
  • If the Bench Trial is to proceed, the Judge will be lenient on both sides in terms of preparation and opening and closing statements.
  • The purpose of a bench trial is for a fast resolution of the criminal case
  • A Judge has final say if a case is not suitable for a Bench Trial - they do not have to give a reasoning - it is noted however that judges thoroughly enjoy Bench Trials and will always try and accommodate any requests

Z. Court Process: Sentencing Hearings

If a Defendant has entered a GUILTY plea on a Capital case, they will be required to have a sentencing hearing in front of a Judge to determine the Jail time and any fine that is due. This is completed because there is a significant range of time and fine that can be given in these cases and the Judiciary are required to hear all evidence and arguments before making a ruling. Any defendant in this situation will be placed on a HUT (Hold Until Trial) until a scheduled date has been arranged at the earliest opportunity. This case could be heard immediately (if all parties available) or could take up to 10-15 days (people's availability being the main factor).

The sequence of a sentencing hearing is similar to that of a court case, however the emphasis is not on the need for the state to prove beyond a reasonable doubt, however in proving the Judge with the information to make an informed decision in regards to time and fine.

Opening of Court: The presiding Judge will open court as per usual, the state and defense will confirm representation and the defendant will be confirmed as present. The defendant will be asked if they are familiar with the sequence of events and if not, the judge will provide an overview.

Confirmation of charges and Plea: The state will present the charges as the defendant pled Guilty to and the defendant will be asked to confirm the plea - it is noted that the defendant can choose at this time to change to a not Guilty Plea. If this happens, the state and defense will be asked if they are ready to convert the hearing to a criminal case and if so it will proceed as such. It is much more likely that they will not be ready and the defendant will then be remanded back to prison until a new date is available and all stages of the Pre Trial process can be concluded.

Opening Statements: The State will begin with an opening statement, highlighting the key points they wish to make, then the defense will do the same

States Presentation of Evidence: The state will provide any key evidence to the Judge, this may include, but is not limited to - Witness Statements, Impact Statements, Witness testimony, Police reports, Physical evidence. Any Witness called to the stand to give testimony may not be cross examined by the Defense

Defenses Presentation of Evidence: The Defense may choose to call their own witness to counteract any evidence from the state - this is usually, however reserved for character witness for the defense. Unusually the Defendant may also speak on their own behalf. Should they choose to do so, they may be questioned by the Judge - this is the only opportunity when a Judge may do so.

Recommendation for sentencing: When evidence has been given by both sides, the state and defense will be asked to provide a recommendation for sentence. This is an opportunity for the state to either push for a harsher sentence or ask for leniency. The defense will typically ask for leniency and will have provided factors for the judge to take into account

Reaching a Sentence: The Judge will retire to chambers for a short period to consider the information presented and determine a suitable amount. This is completed in the same was as deliberation in a criminal case, the only exception is that the Guilt is not in question

Overriding the Charges: The role of the Judiciary is to ensure that justice prevails, if for any reason they believe that the charges that have been put forward are incorrect/harsh/unconstitutional they do have the ability to find the defendant guilty on a lesser charge. This is a very unusual situation, but can occur.

Reading the Ruling: The Judge will return to the Courtroom and will provide the sentence, this is in accordance with the same as in a criminal court setting. At this point, the court is closed and no further discussion takes place.

[. Court Process: The Appellate Court

The appellate court, also known as an appeals court or court of appeals, is a higher court that reviews decisions made by lower courts (district court). Its primary function is to hear appeals filed by parties who are dissatisfied with the judgments or rulings issued by trial courts or lower tribunals. Appellate courts do not conduct trials or hear testimony from witnesses; instead, they focus on reviewing the legal arguments, procedures, and decisions made by lower courts to determine if any judicial errors occurred.

They tend to focus on the following:

Reviewing Decisions: Appellate courts review the decisions made by trial courts or lower tribunals to determine if legal errors were made. This includes reviewing issues related to the interpretation and application of the law, procedural errors, and constitutional issues.

Legal Arguments: Appellate courts primarily consider legal arguments presented by the parties, rather than reexamining the facts or evidence of the case. Attorneys for the parties submit written briefs outlining their legal arguments, and in some cases, may also present oral arguments before a panel of judges. The Appellant presents legal arguments to the panel, in writing, in a document called a "brief." In the brief, the appellant tries to persuade the Justices that the trial court made an error, and that its decision should be reversed. This must be completed within 28 days of the conviction. Appeals may be submitted after the fact for Guilty Verdicts and the Judges will only decide if proper procedure has been followed, NOT overturn a verdict because of new evidence submission. The party defending against the appeal, known as the "appellee," may submit a brief to show why the trial court decision was correct, or why any error made by the trial court was not significant enough to affect the outcome of the case. If the Judges need more information a date will be scheduled to hear the arguments from both parties whereas questions may be asked by the Judges.

Panel of Judges: Appellate cases are typically heard by a panel of judges, rather than a single judge. The number of judges on the panel varies depending on the complexity of the case and the number of Judges available. In some cases, appellate courts may also hear cases “en banc”, meaning that all the judges/justices on the court hear the case together.

Types of Appeals: Appellate courts may hear different types of appeals, including appeals from civil cases or criminal cases

Standard of Review: Appellate courts apply a standard of review to evaluate the decisions made by lower courts. This standard determines the level of deference given to the lower court's findings and may vary depending on the type of issue being reviewed.

Deliberation: The Judges will then proceed to Deliberate, and return a decision typically within 48 hours - although they do have up to 14 days to make a decision on an appeal.

Types of Relief: Depending on the outcome of the appeal, appellate courts may affirm the lower court's decision, reverse it, remand the case back to the lower court for further proceedings, or modify the judgment in some way.

Precedent: Appellate court decisions can establish legal precedent that guides future cases. These precedents help to ensure consistency and predictability in the application of the law.

6. Civil Administration - The Court Process

A. Court Process: The Purpose of Civil Court

The purpose of civil court is to resolve disputes between individuals, businesses, organizations, or other entities in matters that typically involve private rights, duties, and obligations. Unlike criminal court, where the focus is on punishing individuals for violations of criminal law, civil court deals with non-criminal matters and seeks to provide remedies for individuals who have been wronged or harmed by the actions of others. Key purposes include:

Adjudicating Civil Disputes: Civil court serves as a forum for resolving civil disputes between parties who have disagreements over various issues such as contracts, property rights, personal injuries, family matters, business disputes, landlord-tenant disputes, and more.

Enforcing Legal Rights: Civil court allows individuals to enforce their legal rights and seek remedies for violations of those rights. This may include seeking compensation for damages, obtaining injunctions to stop harmful actions, or seeking specific performance of contractual obligations.

Providing Redress for Harm: Civil court provides a means for individuals who have suffered harm or injury due to the actions or negligence of others to seek redress and obtain compensation for their losses. This can include compensation for property damage, lost wages, pain and suffering, and other damages.

Interpreting and Applying Civil Laws: Civil court interprets and applies civil laws, statutes, regulations, and legal principles to resolve disputes and provide guidance on the rights and obligations of parties involved in civil matters. Court decisions in civil cases help to clarify and establish legal precedent for future cases.

Preserving Order and Stability: Civil court plays a role in preserving social order and stability by providing a peaceful and orderly process for resolving disputes between parties. It helps to prevent individuals from taking the law into their own hands and promotes the rule of law.

Protecting Individual Rights: Civil court protects individuals' rights by providing a fair and impartial forum for the resolution of disputes. It ensures that parties have the opportunity to present their cases, confront witnesses, and receive a reasoned decision from a neutral adjudicator.

Promoting Settlement and Resolution: Civil court encourages parties to resolve their disputes through negotiation, mediation, or settlement whenever possible. Court-mandated alternative dispute resolution processes help parties reach mutually acceptable agreements without the need for a full trial.

Overall, civil court serves as an essential component of the legal system by providing a mechanism for individuals to seek justice, resolve disputes, and obtain remedies for civil wrongs. It helps to ensure fairness, equity, and accountability in society by adjudicating civil matters in a transparent and impartial manner

B. Civil Court Process: Preparing for Court

The process for the civil courts starts with the submission of a civil complaint to the court. Civil lawsuits are to be submitted through the Civil Court Dockets, this is called a civil claim. Typically a claim should be made within 7 days of the dispute, however is not a requirement. Civil complaints can be filed with the courts by private individuals, private businesses or the state itself and can be actions against other private individuals, private businesses or the state itself.

The civil courts are not for submitting criminal reports or complaints. If a private individual or business believes a crime has been committed they should report this to local law enforcement for investigation.

In civil court, the representatives of the individual or enterprise that lodged the complaint are known as the plaintiff. It is the job of the plaintiff or their representation to put their case to the courts and provide evidence of their claim against the accused entity. The Plaintiff must prove on a balance of probabilities in their favor that their claim is valid.

The accused and their representatives are known as the respondent, they are to respond to the claims made against them and provide justification or defense of those actions to argue their case, they must prove their case on the preponderance of the evidence between those involved in the civil action.

In a civil case, the burden of proof lies on both parties to prove their arguments and cases. The weight of the burden is based on the preponderance of the evidence. The preponderance of the evidence is much less than beyond a reasonable doubt, as you just need to prove it was more likely than not. Under the preponderance standard, the burden of proof is met when the party with the burden convinces the fact finder that there is a greater than 50% chance that the claim is true.

After a case has been initiated, the Plaintiff/Petitioner and the Respondent may reach a SETTLEMENT AGREEMENT before going to the hearing, and furthermore, up until a judgment is determined. It is required that the parties inform the Court on the SETTLEMENT immediately in either situation so the Court can close the case.

After the initial pleadings are filed, the parties may engage in pre-trial motions and pleadings, such as motions to dismiss, motions for summary judgment, or motions to compel discovery. These motions are aimed at resolving legal issues or narrowing the scope of the case before trial

The case will then move into Discovery - like in a criminal case - this is the process by which parties obtain information and evidence from each other to prepare for trial. This may include interrogatories (written questions), requests for documents or records, depositions (sworn testimony under oath), and requests for admissions.

If the case is required to go to a hearing, a date will be organized by the presiding Judge, who will make best efforts to arrange a time suitable for all parties. It is noted that civil cases are of a lower priority to criminal cases and as such may take longer. In addition, a civil case can not be raised until any impacts from a linked criminal case are fully known and a verdict has been reached in the criminal case.

C. Civil Court Process: Arrival at Court

All parties are required to arrive at Court no later than 10 minutes before the scheduled start time. All private citizens will be searched before they are granted entry to the Courtroom. It is important to note that if you have any items that may be used as a weapon you will not be allowed to enter. Additionally, masks must all be removed unless you have a medical exemption provided by SAHA. Suitable clothing is required to be worn - no beachwear..

Law Enforcement Officers & members of the Department of Corrections are the only persons allowed to enter with their firearms.

When you enter the courtroom you find your seats and await the commencement of proceedings. The session will start with all members present - the Plaintiff and the respondent, security and the Judiciary. When court is in session you must remain calm and quiet, if any person is a disruption they will be removed from the courtroom and may be held in contempt of court.

The upstairs gallery is reserved for government personnel and security detail only.

Should you wish to leave the courtroom at any time, a member of the security detail will escort you out. You will not be able to return unless you are searched again. As court can be a long process, it is highly recommended that you have food and drink available to you.

D. Civil Court Process: Court Procedure

If the case has not been settled, it proceeds to trial. At trial, both parties have the opportunity to present evidence, call witnesses, and make legal arguments. The trial will follow a structured format consisting of:

Opening Statements: Plaintiff is first with the opening statement, followed by the Respondent

Presentation of Evidence: The Plaintiff presents witnesses and evidence during DIRECT Examination

and the Respondent is allowed to CROSS Examine said witness. The Plaintiff then has the opportunity to re-DIRECT, and Respondent re-CROSS examine (different to in a criminal case). This sequence of events will continue until all witnesses have been called. It is noted that the Judge may intervene and ask questions for clarification to any party in attendance or who takes the witness stand. The Respondent then has the opportunity to call witnesses - the same pattern will be repeated of Direct Examination - Cross Examination - Redirect and Re-cross

Closing Arguments: Plaintiff is first with the closing statement, followed by the Respondent.

Deliberation and Judgment:After the conclusion of the trial, the judge deliberates on the evidence and legal arguments presented and reaches a decision. The judge will then issue its judgment.

Awards: Awards in civil cases, also referred to as damages, are determined based on various factors, including the nature of the case, the evidence presented, and applicable legal principles. The process of deciding awards in civil cases typically involves the following steps

Types of Damages: Before determining awards, it's important to understand the types of damages that may be awarded in civil cases:

  • Compensatory Damages: These are intended to compensate the plaintiff for actual losses or harm suffered as a result of the defendant's actions. Compensatory damages can include economic damages (such as lost wages, property damage) and non-economic damages (such as pain and suffering, emotional distress).
  • Punitive Damages: In cases involving egregious conduct or intentional wrongdoing, punitive damages may be awarded to punish the defendant and deter similar behavior in the future. Punitive damages are intended to punish the defendant rather than compensate the plaintiff for specific losses.

Factors Considered: In determining the amount of damages, various factors may be considered, including:

  • The nature and extent of the plaintiff's injuries or losses.
  • The degree of fault or misconduct on the part of the defendant.
  • Any aggravating or mitigating circumstances relevant to the case.
  • Comparable awards in similar cases or precedent set by previous court decisions.
  • Statutory limits or caps on damages that may apply in certain types of cases. The maximum amount that can be awarded in total of both types of damages stands at $750,000

Post-Trial Motions: After a judgment is entered, either party may file post-trial motions, such as motions for a new trial or motions to set aside the judgment. These motions seek to challenge the outcome of the trial based on legal or procedural grounds.

A. Parole

Parole is a system of supervised release granted to individuals who have been incarcerated for a crime before they have served their full sentence. The purpose of parole is to reintegrate offenders back into society under supervision while also ensuring public safety. Parole in Los Santos is managed by the PaP team in the department of corrections - Here's how parole works and its main purposes:

Reintegration into Society: One of the primary purposes of parole is to facilitate the reintegration of offenders into society after a period of incarceration. By granting parole, the DOJ aim to provide offenders with the opportunity to rebuild their lives, establish positive relationships, find employment, and contribute to their communities.

Gradual Reentry: Parole allows offenders to transition from prison to the community gradually. Rather than being released abruptly at the end of their sentence, parole provides a structured process for individuals to reintegrate into society while still being monitored and supervised by parole officers.

Supervision and Support: Parolees are typically required to adhere to certain conditions of release, such as regularly reporting to a parole officer, participating in rehabilitative programs, abstaining from drugs and alcohol, and avoiding contact with known criminals. Parole officers provide supervision and support to help parolees successfully adjust to life outside of prison and comply with the conditions of their release.

Risk Management: Parole serves as a mechanism for managing the risk posed by offenders who are released from prison before completing their full sentence. Parole boards assess the risk factors associated with individual offenders and make decisions about whether to grant parole and under what conditions. The goal is to balance public safety with the rehabilitation and reintegration needs of the offender.

Incentive for Rehabilitation: Parole can serve as an incentive for offenders to participate in rehabilitative programs and demonstrate good behavior while incarcerated. By meeting certain criteria and showing evidence of rehabilitation, offenders may become eligible for parole earlier than if they were to serve their full sentence.

Conditional Release: Parole is a form of conditional release, meaning that parolees must comply with specific conditions set by the parole board or parole officer. Failure to comply with these conditions can result in parole violations, which may lead to sanctions such as increased supervision, re-incarceration, or revocation of parole.

Requesting Parole: The opportunity to have parole is granted by the judiciary at the point of sentencing, they will provide a timeline at which point it may be possible to request a hearing, however, if during the period of incarceration the behavior of the convicted is not suitable the request may be turned down. A hearing will not guarantee that parole is granted. For inmates who have transferred in from another prison there is a standard waiting time before they may request a hearing - this is subject to change and as such please contact a member of the Judiciary for the latest timelines

B. Restitution

Restitution in criminal court cases refers to the court-ordered repayment or compensation to a defendant when they have been found NOT GUILTY of the crimes they were tried for. This is not guaranteed and is only issued as a good faith from the Court or the District Attorney's office.

The current rate of restitution is from $0-$100 per month of incarcerated time - it is capped to a maximum of $150,000 for any single court case.

C. Restraining Orders

A restraining order, also known as a protective order or an order of protection, is a legal order issued by a court to protect an individual from harassment, abuse, threats, or violence by another person. Restraining orders are used in situations where someone feels threatened or unsafe due to the actions or behavior of another individual. They are designed to provide a legal mechanism for victims of harassment or abuse to seek protection and prevent further harm.

The primary purpose of a restraining order is to provide protection to the victim and prevent further harm or violence. Restraining orders may prohibit the restrained person from contacting or coming near the protected person, their home, workplace, or other specified locations. They may also include other protective provisions, such as prohibiting harassment, threats, or acts of violence.

To obtain a temporary restraining order, the victim typically files a petition with any law Enforcement officer outlining the reasons for seeking protection and providing evidence of the harassment or abuse. The LEO may grant a temporary restraining order (TRO) which will be valid for 7 days.

Should the behavior continue, the victim may then file to the court for a Full Restraining Order. If the court finds sufficient evidence of harassment or abuse, it may issue a permanent restraining order for a specified duration, which can last from 7day to 6 months initially.

Violating a restraining order is a serious offense and can result in legal consequences for the restrained person, including fines, arrest, and criminal charges. Law enforcement agencies are responsible for enforcing restraining orders and may take immediate action to protect the victim if the order is violated.

D. Charge Stacking

Charge stacking, also known as charge bundling or overcharging, refers to the practice of prosecutors or Law Enforcement Officers bringing multiple criminal charges against a defendant for a single act or offense. It involves the filing of multiple charges that may overlap or be duplicative, often resulting in the defendant facing a greater number of charges than warranted by the circumstances of the case. Charge stacking can have significant implications for defendants, potentially leading to harsher penalties, increased legal costs, and longer court proceedings.

Here are some key points to think about in regards to Charge Stacking

Multiplicity of Charges: Charge stacking involves bringing multiple charges against a defendant for what could be considered a single criminal act or incident. This may include charging the defendant with multiple offenses that stem from the same conduct or incident, such as assault and battery, theft and receiving stolen property, or drug possession and possession with intent to distribute.

Enhancing Prosecutorial Leverage: Prosecutors may engage in charge stacking as a strategy to enhance their leverage in plea negotiations or to secure a more favorable outcome at trial. By bringing multiple charges with potentially severe penalties, prosecutors may exert pressure on defendants to plead guilty to lesser offenses or to cooperate with law enforcement in exchange for reduced charges or leniency. We do not encourage this behavior at all and do monitor for it.

Increased Penalties: Charge stacking can result in defendants facing significantly harsher penalties if convicted on multiple charges. Each individual charge may carry its own set of penalties, including fines, probation, or incarceration, and these penalties may be imposed consecutively, leading to longer overall sentences.

Legal Challenges: Defendants and their attorneys may challenge charge stacking on various grounds, such as arguing that the charges are duplicative, violate double jeopardy protections, or constitute prosecutorial misconduct. Courts may dismiss or consolidate charges if they determine that the prosecution has engaged in improper charge stacking practices.

Fairness and Due Process Concerns: Charge stacking raises concerns about fairness and due process, as it may result in defendants facing a disproportionate level of criminal liability relative to their actions or culpability. Critics argue that charge stacking can lead to excessive punishment and undermine the principles of proportionality and justice in the criminal justice system.

F. Conflicts of Interest

A conflict of interest in a court case occurs when a person involved in the legal proceedings has competing interests or loyalties that could potentially bias their judgment, decisions, or actions, and undermine their ability to fulfill their duties impartially and ethically. Conflict of interest situations can arise in various contexts within the legal system, involving judges, attorneys, parties, witnesses, jurors, and other participants.

The most common examples of conflicts of interest in court cases:

Attorney-Client Conflict: A conflict may arise if an attorney's personal interests or obligations conflict with the interests of their client. For example, an attorney may have a personal relationship with someone involved in the case, have a financial interest in the outcome, or have represented conflicting parties in related matters.

Conflicts Among Co-Defendants: In cases where multiple defendants are represented by the same attorney or law firm, conflicts may arise if the defendants have conflicting interests or defenses. This can create a conflict of interest for the attorney, who must prioritize the interests of one client over another.

Judicial Conflict: Judges are expected to be impartial and unbiased in their decision-making. However, conflicts of interest may arise if a judge has a personal or financial interest in the outcome of the case, has a relationship with one of the parties or attorneys, or has previously been involved in the case in another capacity.

Witness Conflicts: Witnesses may have conflicts of interest if their testimony could implicate themselves or someone with whom they have a relationship. Additionally, witnesses may have personal or financial interests that conflict with the interests of the party they are testifying for or against.

Prosecutorial Conflict: Prosecutors have a duty to seek justice and represent the interests of the public. However, conflicts of interest may arise if a prosecutor has a personal or professional relationship with a party involved in the case, or if the prosecutor has a personal stake in the outcome.

Conflicts of interest can undermine the fairness and integrity of court proceedings, potentially leading to biased decisions, compromised ethical standards, and miscarriages of justice. It is essential for participants in the legal system to identify and address conflicts of interest promptly to ensure that court proceedings are conducted fairly and impartially. Measures such as disclosure, recusal, and disqualification may be necessary to mitigate conflicts of interest and uphold the integrity of the legal process. If any member of the Department of Justice does not declare a Conflict of Interest and it is found to have interfered in their public duty, they will be terminated and the bar card removed.

G. Seizure of Assets

The Court can order the seizure of assets in various legal contexts, typically when there is a legal basis for doing so and when it is deemed necessary to enforce a judgment, satisfy a debt, or preserve assets pending the outcome of legal proceedings.

Enforcement of Judgments: After obtaining a judgment in a civil lawsuit, a prevailing party may seek to enforce the judgment by seizing the assets of the losing party. This could involve seizing bank accounts, real estate, vehicles, or other valuable property owned by the debtor to satisfy the judgment debt.

Collection of Unpaid Debts: Creditors who are owed money by a debtor may seek a court order to seize the debtor's assets as a means of collecting on the unpaid debt. This could include garnishing wages, levying bank accounts, or placing liens on property owned by the debtor.

Criminal Forfeiture: In criminal cases involving financial crimes or illegal activities, such as drug trafficking or fraud, courts may order the seizure and forfeiture of assets that were obtained through unlawful means or used in the commission of the crime. This could include seizing cash, vehicles, real estate, or other assets connected to the criminal activity.

Prejudgment Remedies: In certain cases where there is a risk that a defendant may attempt to conceal or dissipate assets to avoid paying a judgment, courts may issue prejudgment remedies to preserve the defendant's assets pending the outcome of legal proceedings. This could involve freezing bank accounts, placing liens on property, or other measures to prevent the dissipation of assets.

It's important to note that the seizure of assets is subject to legal procedures and safeguards to protect the rights of individuals whose assets are being seized. Defendants and debtors have the right to due process, including notice and an opportunity to be heard, before their assets can be seized by the court. Additionally, there may be limitations on the types of assets that can be seized and the circumstances under which seizure is permissible, depending on applicable laws and regulations.

H. Mental Health Evaluations

Courts may become involved in mental health evaluations in several situations where mental health issues are relevant to legal proceedings or the well-being of individuals.

Competency to Stand Trial: If there are doubts about a defendant's mental competency to understand the charges against them or to assist in their own defense, the court may order a mental health evaluation to assess the defendant's competency. This evaluation helps determine whether the defendant is mentally fit to stand trial.

Insanity Defense: In cases where a defendant raises an insanity defense, claiming that they were not criminally responsible for their actions due to a mental illness or defect, the court may order a mental health evaluation to assess the defendant's mental state at the time of the offense.

Guardianship or Conservatorship Proceedings: In civil cases involving guardianship or conservatorship proceedings, where individuals may be deemed unable to manage their own affairs due to mental incapacity, the court may order a mental health evaluation to assess the individual's mental capacity and determine whether guardianship or conservatorship is warranted.

Involuntary Commitment: In cases where individuals pose a danger to themselves or others due to mental illness, the court may order an involuntary mental health evaluation to determine whether the individual should be involuntarily committed to a psychiatric facility for treatment and stabilization. In these situations, any Law Enforcement Officer or Medic may place the individual on a temporary hold - a 51/50 (see table below for all types of holds and responsibilities)

Civil Commitment Proceedings: In cases where individuals are considered a danger to themselves or others due to mental illness but have not committed a crime, the court may order a mental health evaluation as part of civil commitment proceedings to determine whether involuntary commitment to a psychiatric facility is necessary for treatment and safety reasons.

In each of these scenarios, the involvement of the court in ordering a mental health evaluation is aimed at addressing legal issues, ensuring the fair treatment of individuals involved, and protecting the safety and well-being of the community. Mental health evaluations conducted at the direction of the court will involve a comprehensive assessment by qualified mental health professionals to provide the court with relevant information and recommendations for decision-making.
